What the number means

A standard plane here is a mid-sagittal transperineal view in which the pubic symphysis is fully visible along its long axis and the fetal skull contour is resolved — the condition for measuring angle of progression or head–symphysis distance. Frames that are off-midline, blurred by probe transition, or showing only one of the two structures are non-standard.

The threshold is not 0.5. It was chosen on the validation split to maximise macro-F1 and then frozen before the test split was touched, which is why it sits where it does.

Where this model will mislead you. It was trained only on third-trimester intrapartum transperineal video from three hospitals. On a first-trimester scan, a transabdominal view, a different probe, or any non-ultrasound image, it still returns a confident number — that number is meaningless. There is no "out of distribution" output.

The study behind it

Published work reports that models trained on dense video frames beat models trained on curated still-image datasets, and reads that as evidence that video data is richer. The dense arm also has far more training samples, and the two explanations have not been separated.

This project separates them by building both arms from one corpus, varying only how many frames are drawn per video, so probe, anatomy, scanners, annotators and label definition are constant by construction. A matched-budget arm then caps the dense arm to the sparse arm's exact frame count, and a prior-matched arm additionally holds the class balance fixed.

What it shows so far

Training on 124× more frames does not help. The sparse arm spans 0.588–0.687 macro-F1 across 434 to 8,638 frames with no trend; the dense arm spans 0.506–0.637 across 6,840 to 53,996 frames, also with no trend, and does not beat the sparse arm even in Protocol A — the comparison the literature reports as a win for video.

Under matched budget the dense arm loses all four times, and holding the class prior fixed as well does not rescue it. The dense matched arm sees roughly 20–25 distinct patients against the sparse arm's 434, which points at patient diversity rather than frame count.

One seed. Do not over-read this. The spread within each arm (about 0.10–0.13 macro-F1) is as large as the gaps between arms, so none of it is yet distinguishable from run-to-run noise. Repeat seeds and a paired video bootstrap are what would turn this into a claim; until then it is a direction, not a result.

One thing that is not subtle: absolute performance sits far below the 0.83–0.96 the protocol anticipated from simulation. That is the train/test shift doing its work — the model is trained on trimmed clips where the plane is held throughout, and tested on sweeps that move in and out of plane.

Post-hoc smoothing, expected to be a strong baseline, hurt in 12 of the 13 conditions. The smoother is tuned on validation, where standard-plane runs are long (median 33 frames), then applied to test, where they are short (median 12). A filter tuned on the wrong run-length distribution over-smooths.

Does temporal context help?

The second arm puts a BiLSTM on the same encoder, trained on the same dense_all frames. The training split contains no label transitions at all, so transitions were synthesised by splicing positive and negative clips together — otherwise the temporal model never observes the one event it exists to model.

The temporal arm wins, and it beats the smoothed baseline the protocol expected to be hard to beat. But splicing is not what produced that. The unspliced model — which cannot have learned transitions, because its training windows contain none — reaches 0.6496, and the intervals overlap heavily. So whatever the temporal head contributes here, it is not transition modelling; most likely it is the sliding-window logit averaging at inference, which is itself a form of smoothing.

What went wrong on the way here

Four defects in data preparation, none of which raised an error. They are written up in full in the repository's data audit because the failure mode — a pipeline that runs to completion and reports wrong numbers — is the reusable lesson.

  • The ALL sentinel parsed to nothing. The label CSV marks whole-video positives with the string ALL. It is not a Python literal, so it fell through to a digit scan, matched nothing, and every one of the 266 standard-plane training videos contributed zero labels.
  • 266 filenames are doubled in the published archive. The videos ship as X__X.avi while the label file calls them X.avi. Those are the same 266 videos, so two independent bugs destroyed every positive training video at once.
  • 71 videos silently extracted nothing. cv2.imwrite encodes paths with the Windows ANSI codepage and reports failure by returning False. The videos with Chinese characters in their filenames decoded, were counted into the manifest, and wrote zero files.
  • The training split contains no label transitions at all. Train is trimmed clips — 0.00 transitions per video, against 1.35 in test. A temporal model trained there reaches zero loss by ignoring time, so the temporal arm needed synthesised transitions to be measurable.
